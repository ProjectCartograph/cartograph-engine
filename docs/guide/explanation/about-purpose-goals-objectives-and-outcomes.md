# About purpose, goals, objectives and outcomes

A reasonable question is why Cartograph asks for a purpose, then goals, then objectives, then outcomes, when "what do we want to achieve?" sounds like one question. This page explains why there are four, and why they come in that order. It uses the produce cooperative in Cartograph's example, whose members grow produce and sell it together through six depots.

## Four questions, not one

They answer different questions.

- **Purpose** is why the organisation exists: the future it wants for the people it serves (its vision), and what it does to get there (its mission). The cooperative's vision is that every member family earns a fair and dependable living from what it grows.
- A **goal** is a broad direction the organisation keeps working towards. The cooperative has one called "Raise produce quality".
- An **objective** is one concrete change it sets out to make under a goal. Under that goal sits "Hold every depot to one standard".
- An **outcome** is a fact about people or things once that change has happened. It is a state, not a task. Under that objective sit "Depot staff apply the standard alike" and "Faults are found before produce leaves the depot".

Ask "what do we do?" and you get tasks. Ask "what will be true?" and you get outcomes. Keeping the two apart is the main reason for the levels. A plan that lists "train depot staff in grading" among its goals mixes an action with a result. Whether the training works shows up only if the result is written down on its own, as the cooperative does: "The same delivery gets the same verdict whoever checks it."

A broad aim at the top with more specific aims beneath it is how many results frameworks are built. Cartograph's own design notes ([TAXONOMY.md](../../TAXONOMY.md), decision D25) say which frameworks it took from. Cartograph calls the three levels goal, objective and outcome. A workspace can label them in the words its plan uses, such as "priority" or "pillar".

## Where a gap fits

A gap is the distance between how things are and how they should be, on one measure. It is not a problem and it is not a missing tool.

Cartograph asks for gaps because people find it easier to agree on what is wrong than on what to promise. In the cooperative, the gap is "Faults are found after dispatch": one fault in five is found at intake, and buyers find the rest. The outcome that closes it is "Faults are found before produce leaves the depot". One outcome can close several gaps.

Cartograph's design notes write the outcome first, as the state the purpose calls for, and name the gap after it ([TAXONOMY.md](../../TAXONOMY.md), decisions D24 and D28). The walk that starts a project asks for the gaps before the outcomes, because that is the order people tend to think of them in.

## Why indicators hang off outcomes

An outcome without a measure is a wish. An indicator gives it a baseline, a target and a date. In the cooperative, "Quality pass rate" is the share of deliveries meeting the quality standard at intake. It starts at 62 percent in September 2025, with a target of 75 percent by June 2027.

Cartograph also shows five marks on every goal, objective and outcome, based on SMART: specific, measurable, attainable, relevant and time-bound ([TAXONOMY.md](../../TAXONOMY.md), decision D25). The marks are read from the record and never typed, so they say honestly what is missing: a measure with no baseline is not yet attainable, and a target with no date is not yet time-bound.

## Why the work comes last

Projects, programmes and services are the means. Each says which outcomes it serves. In the cooperative, the Quality Check Rollout project works towards the outcomes above. This is why you meet your strategy first: when someone later asks "why are we doing this project?", the answer is already a link.

## Portfolios, programmes and projects

The means come in three sizes. A project delivers. A programme brings
several projects, and sometimes services, together to make one change. A
portfolio does something else: it ranks work and decides what to fund.
The levels also differ in what they point at. A programme and a project
serve outcomes. A portfolio is ranked against goals and objectives, and
Cartograph does not let you pick an outcome there, because an outcome is
what is true afterwards, not what the organisation sets out to do. For
the full account, see [About programmes, portfolios and
projects](about-programmes-portfolios-and-projects.md).

## What this costs

It asks for more structure than a to-do list does. A small group with one project may find four levels heavy. Cartograph lets you skip a step with **Skip for now** and come back, and the setup walk asks for just enough to start. The trade is that an organisation which writes these down once can show, years later, how a piece of work connects to its purpose.

There is also a limit worth stating: a project does not produce benefits by itself. It produces outputs. Benefits come when outputs change what people do. The Association for Project Management makes the same point when it describes [programmes as coordinating projects to deliver beneficial change](https://www.apm.org.uk/resources/what-is-project-management/what-is-programme-management/).
