# About project charters

A project charter is a short document that lets someone with authority say yes to a piece of work. Most standards use that idea. They give it different names (a charter, a brief, a business case) and they disagree about how much goes in it. This page explains what a charter is for, what the standards agree on, and how Cartograph's project walk follows them and where it goes its own way.

It is a discussion, not a set of steps. To define a project, follow [the tutorial](../tutorials/charter-your-first-project.md). To look up a record's fields, see [Kinds of record](../reference/kinds-of-record.md).

## What a charter is for

Think of the produce cooperative in the example vault. Its members want every depot to check deliveries against one standard. Before anyone spends money on that, somebody has to decide: is this worth doing, who is in charge of it, and what are we allowed to spend? A charter is the document that decision rests on.

Two jobs follow from that.

- **It lets a decision-maker say yes or no.** The PRINCE2 method calls its version a project brief, and its board uses the brief to decide whether to authorise the next stage ([PRINCE2 wiki, project brief](https://prince2.wiki/management-products/project-brief/)). The UK government's project delivery standard says each stage is preceded by a gate that authorises the next one ([GovS 002, clause 6.3](https://projectdelivery.gov.uk/library-products/government-functional-standard-govs-002-project-delivery/)).
- **It gives the team something to come back to.** PRINCE2 keeps the brief's successor, the project initiation documentation, as a baseline to measure progress and to ask whether the project is still worth doing ([PRINCE2 wiki, initiation documentation](https://prince2.wiki/management-products/project-initiation-documentation/)).

The PRINCE2 wiki is a community site, not the publisher's manual, so treat its lists as a good summary rather than the text of the method.

## What the standards agree on

The sources do not use one list. Read side by side, seven things turn up in all of them.

| What | In plain words | Where you can see it |
|---|---|---|
| Why | The need or the problem. | The PRINCE2 brief's background; the Green Book's [case for change](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a4390675b6406df58c14006/Guidance_on_Developing_Business_Cases.pdf); the GOV.UK Service Manual's [reframed problem](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-discovery-phase-works) |
| What will change | Objectives you could check. | The Green Book asks for SMART objectives, as few as possible |
| How you will know | Success criteria, benefits or indicators. | The European Commission's [logical framework](https://caricom.org/wp-content/uploads/EC_PCM_Training_Handbook.pdf) has a column for them |
| Scope | What is in, and what is left out. | The Inception Deck's "not list"; the PRINCE2 product description |
| Who | Stakeholders, and who is accountable. | PRINCE2's brief; the Green Book's management case |
| What could stop it | Risks and constraints. | The Green Book strategic case; GOV.UK discovery splits constraints into hard and soft |
| Who says yes | A named authority. | GovS 002 clause 6.4.5: the senior responsible owner confirms the need is real |

The Project Management Institute's older charter list, as an open university textbook reports it, has the same core plus a few more: purpose, measurable objectives and success criteria, a high-level description with boundaries and key deliverables, assumptions and constraints, overall risk, a summary milestone schedule, pre-approved financial resources, key stakeholders, approval requirements, exit criteria, and the manager and sponsor with their authority ([Oguz, Project Management, chapter 3.1](https://pressbooks.ulib.csuohio.edu/projectmanagement2ndedition/chapter/3-1-project-charter/)). The Institute's own pages were not available to check, so this list is second-hand, and it describes the sixth edition of the PMBOK Guide.

## What is common but optional

Beyond those seven, sources split.

- **Deliverables, a rough schedule and a rough budget.** Almost everyone asks for them in some form, but as a summary, not a plan.
- **Assumptions and dependencies.** The logical framework gives assumptions a column of their own. GovS 002 says unknown values should be written down as assumptions in the business case (clause 7.6) and that dependencies between pieces of work should be defined (clause 7.2.4).
- **Benefits.** PRINCE2's business case and GovS 002 both ask for each benefit to have a measure, and GovS 002 gives each one an owner (clause 7.3).
- **Options you considered, including doing nothing.** The Green Book's economic case and PRINCE2's business case both ask for this. [APM's page on the business case](https://www.apm.org.uk/resources/what-is-project-management/what-is-a-business-case/) says the approved business case keeps the do-nothing option as a reference.
- **Roles and governance.** Who sits on which board, and who is consulted.

## What belongs to later planning

The sources keep a second layer of detail out of the first document, on purpose.

- The detailed plan and its costs. PRINCE2 puts the project plan in the initiation documentation, after the brief. The Green Book spells the financial and management cases out fully only at the outline and full business case stages, not in the strategic outline case.
- Approach documents for quality, risk, communication and change.
- Investment appraisal, such as net present value.
- Contracts and procurement.
- The list of tasks under each deliverable.

The shape this gives is two questions asked in order. First: should we start, and who says so? Then: how, when and at what cost? A charter answers the first.

## Small work, large work, agile and staged work

None of the sources tells you a size at which to switch forms. The Green Book says a business case should match the scale, cost, complexity and risk of the proposal, and that you should agree with the approving authority up front what kind of case to build (see [paragraphs 3.21 and 3.22](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a4390675b6406df58c14006/Guidance_on_Developing_Business_Cases.pdf)). GovS 002 says simpler projects have fewer stages. The headings stay the same for a hobby project and for a national programme. What changes is the depth, who approves, and how many steps the approval is split into.

How you work changes the shape more than how big you are.

- **Staged, predictive work** fixes its gates. The Green Book moves from a strategic outline case to an outline business case to a full business case. Each stage fills in more of the same five perspectives.
- **Agile work** moves the plan later and the testing earlier. The Inception Deck, described in Jonathan Rasmusson's [The Agile Samurai](https://oreilly.com/library/view/the-agile-samurai/9781680500066/f_0026.html), is ten short questions the whole team answers together, including what is out of scope and what keeps the team up at night. GOV.UK splits the commitment into discovery, alpha and beta, and tests the riskiest assumption first ([discovery](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-discovery-phase-works), [alpha](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-alpha-phase-works)).

Both want the same things at the start: a purpose, the people, the limits, and a way to tell whether it worked.

## How Cartograph's project walk lines up

Cartograph's walk has eight stages: Context, Problem, Objectives, Governance, Scope and Deliverables, Plan, Success and handover, and Approval. Here is how they meet the seven.

| The seven | Where Cartograph asks for it |
|---|---|
| Why | Context (the outcomes it serves) and Problem (who feels it, what is wrong, the change that answers it) |
| What will change | Objectives: one objective, with up to three key results |
| How you will know | Key results, Indicators, and the success criteria in Success and handover |
| Scope | Scope and Deliverables: what is in, what is out, how each output is checked |
| Who | Governance: roles, stakeholders, escalation |
| What could stop it | Plan: the risks, and for each of Scope, Schedule and Cost whether you Hold it, Adjust it first or Concede it |
| Who says yes | Problem records the mandate, the decision, policy or law that authorises the work. Approval records the sign-offs and the conditions |

Some of the match is close to the sources' own words. Cartograph asks for a mandate because PRINCE2 and GovS 002 both start from an authority. Asking which of scope, schedule and cost gives way echoes the Inception Deck's question about what is going to give.

An example: the cooperative's Quality Check Rollout names Raise produce quality as the outcome it serves, a gap called Faults are found after dispatch, one objective with its key results, a Quality Team that owns it, and the Quality Check Service that takes over when it ends.

### Where Cartograph asks for more

The walk also asks about things the standards put later: when the work runs, what each deliverable costs, and what data feeds each indicator, including where it is captured and how often it is reported. Those are plan and data questions. The standards would call them second-stage material.

We ask early for a reason. Cartograph measures results, and an indicator with nowhere to read its figure from would sit empty. The logical framework agrees with the idea (a column for sources of verification) and so does the Green Book's monitoring and evaluation plan. It does not ask for this detail in the charter. If you are not ready to answer these questions, the walk lets you skip them and return.

Cartograph also keeps beneficiary groups without counts. The Green Book asks who benefits and how many. Cartograph asks who, and leaves numbers to indicators, so a group is not frozen at one headcount.

### Where Cartograph asks for less

The sources ask for some things that the project record has no place for.

- **Options considered.** PRINCE2's business case and the Green Book's economic case both ask what else you could do, including nothing. The project record has no field for alternatives. This is a business case question, not strictly a charter one, but it is the largest difference from the two government sources.
- **Disbenefits and benefit timing.** GovS 002 and PRINCE2 ask you to count negative effects. Cartograph asks for the benefit of each intended change, but has no place for a negative effect. A benefit's timing is only recorded through a success criterion that falls due at handover or later.
- **Approach documents.** There is nowhere on a project for a communication or change approach. These are later planning in the sources too.

Assumptions are a third case. The sources list them with the charter. Cartograph keeps each assumption as its own record and links it to the success criterion that depends on it, because an assumption is tested and a risk is mitigated, and a false assumption changes the plan rather than the schedule. That is a design choice, and a reader of the charter may have to follow the link to find them.

### A last difference: one walk, two documents

The sources usually split the document that asks for approval from the document that plans the work: PRINCE2's brief and then its initiation documentation, the Green Book's strategic outline case and then the full case, GOV.UK's discovery and then beta. Cartograph's walk asks for both in one pass, so that a person answers one question at a time instead of facing a long form. The standards would call Plan and its data questions a second stage. You can treat them that way: finish the earlier stages, get a decision, and come back.

## A charter above the project

A programme has a charter too, and it is shaped differently. It opens
with the change the programme exists for and the benefit, then lists the
problems it answers. It lists the indicators the programme is judged by,
with where each starts, its target, its data source and how often it is
read. It prints the theory of change, with the reason for each step, then
the components with a diagram and a line naming the longest chain. Risks,
governance and sign-off close it.

It prints no schedule and no budget. A programme has no dates and no
funding of its own in Cartograph. Its dates are those of the projects
inside it, and its money is theirs.

A project that has components, or is a component of something, prints one
more section, *Components and critical path*, with the same diagram. Its
budget line then reads as the project's own cost together with what its
components cost. See [About components and large
projects](about-components-and-large-projects.md).

## Where this leaves you

A charter does not have to be long. The seven things above fit on a page. Cartograph asks for more because it also helps you plan and measure, and you decide how much of that to do before you ask for a yes.
