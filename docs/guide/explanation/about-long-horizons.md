# About long horizons

A project charter is written once, approved, and read again at a few gates. A programme can last far longer than that, and the people who run it at the end are often not the ones who started it. This page explains what changes when a programme has a long life: how it is defined, how it is reviewed, and why its benefits are tracked after the work has stopped. It also says what Cartograph holds for that today, and what it does not.

It never says how long a programme runs or how often it should be reviewed. Cartograph serves a small cooperative and a national programme alike, and the sources do not give one answer.

## The definition stays open

The first thing that changes is that the definition is not finished when it is approved. HM Treasury's [business case guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a4390675b6406df58c14006/Guidance_on_Developing_Business_Cases.pdf) says a programme business case is not a static document. It stays in force from the first project under it to the last, and is reviewed during that time to confirm that the programme is on track and still good value (clauses 5.7 to 5.15). It asks for a review before each new tranche begins. For UK government departments it sets a minimum interval; that is a rule of that system, not something Cartograph copies.

The UK functional standard [GovS 002](https://projectdelivery.gov.uk/library-products/government-functional-standard-govs-002-project-delivery/) says the same in its own terms: new tranches are planned and reviewed before they are authorised, and the continuing justification for the work is monitored (clause 6.4.6). The National Audit Office's [framework to review programmes](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Framework-to-review-programmes-update-April-2021.pdf) asks whether progress is measured "including consideration that the programme is still the right thing to do" (question 16). A project can be on time and on budget and no longer worth doing. For a programme, the reasons it was started can age.

The Treasury text was read directly. GovS 002 was read through a summarising tool, so its clause numbers are as that tool reported them.

## Tranches are decision points

MSP, APM and the UK government all divide a programme into steps called tranches. GovS 002 defines a tranche as a subdivision of a programme that lets it deliver outputs, outcomes and benefits in increments. [APM's introduction to programme management](https://www.apm.org.uk/media/lm2fdobv/apm-introduction-to-programme-managementsample.pdf) says programmes move through tranches that serve as approval gates and deliver steps in capability.

MSP adds an idea worth keeping. A training provider's summary of the 2011 edition of MSP, which quotes AXELOS under licence, says a tranche is a milestone: the moment when outcomes have been delivered and benefits can start, not just a way to cut up the work ([QA pack](https://www.qa.com/courseware/pcw/pcw-pbpmspp-v4_6.pdf)). The official 5th edition could not be read, so this rests on that summary of the earlier edition.

What the sources share is that a tranche is a **decision point with a reason**. It is where the people accountable look at what has landed, check that the case still holds, and decide what comes next. It is not a calendar slice.

## Benefits fall late, and someone must own them

A programme exists for its benefits, and a benefit often appears long after the work that caused it. The cooperative's Quality Improvement Programme is judged on its quality pass rate. The projects that train depot staff end. The pass rate keeps being read every season for years, by the people who run the quality check service.

Three sources press the same point.

- GovS 002 asks the sponsoring body to review whether benefits and operating performance meet the business case and are likely to keep doing so (clause 6.4.9). It asks the senior responsible owner to agree plans for reviews after closure (clause 6.4.8).
- The UK's [Gate Review 5](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gate-review-5-operations-review-and-benefit-realisation/gate-review-5-operational-review-and-benefits-realisation) asks whether a named owner answers for running the service and for measuring its benefits over the long term, whether the assumptions behind the benefits were tested and updated against results, and whether changes to the baseline went through change control.
- The Infrastructure and Projects Authority's [annual report 2023-24](https://gov.uk/government/publications/infrastructure-and-projects-authority-annual-report-2023-24/infrastructure-and-projects-authority-annual-report-2023-24-html) says only some of the projects in the government's major projects portfolio reported benefits in money, many public-value benefits are hard to put in money, and every project leaving the portfolio is expected to have an exit review that considers tracking its benefits afterwards.

These two pages were read through a summarising tool. The IPA's own benefits guides could not be read, and are not relied on here.

So for a long programme, a benefit needs more than a measure. It needs an owner, a baseline, a sense of when it should start to show, a way of reading it, and something that carries on reading it once the programme has closed.

## Assumptions need a date to be tested

A theory of change says why a programme's work should lead to its change, and what has to be true for each step. On a long programme those assumptions can stop holding. The United Nations Peacebuilding Fund's [guidance note on theory of change](https://www.un.org/peacebuilding/sites/www.un.org.peacebuilding/files/documents/pbf_toc_guidance_note_2021_en.pdf) says assumptions should be made explicit and tested throughout the implementation cycle to see whether they still hold. HM Treasury's [Magenta Book](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a3157a0141f0690ad5fa438/CCS0126982978-001_PN10541411_Magenta_Book_Update_V2_Accessible_2__1_.pdf) says a theory of change should continue to be developed as new evidence arrives. Neither says how often. Both were read directly.

## Plans get reset

When a plan has changed so much that its case has to be made again, the IPA calls it a reset: a significant change to the baseline that needs the business case to be refreshed or changed. The NAO asks whether the business case was reassessed at the programme's strategic decision points and when duration, cost or scope changed significantly (question 5). GovS 002 asks that changes to an approved baseline are controlled and traceable (clause 7.2.5). No source gives a narrower meaning. What they share is that the earlier baseline stays visible, the reason is written down, and the body that approves decides.

## The vision holds while the parts change

A long programme sees projects start, end, merge and be cancelled. What should hold steady is the aim, the theory of change and the benefits. MSP asks for a short vision that many kinds of stakeholder can understand, drafted early and kept up to date. The Treasury lets a project under a programme inherit the strategic case from the programme's case rather than making it again (clause 4.3).

Cartograph's structure supports this. The programme's aim and theory of change sit on the programme. Its components are listed separately, each with the reason, and can change without touching the aim.

## What Cartograph holds today

For a programme with a long life, Cartograph holds:

- **The aim and who gains.** The intended change, and the benefit to someone.
- **A theory of change.** Each step's outcome, what must hold before it, why, and the assumptions it rests on. The Quality Improvement Programme has four steps. The Market Access Programme has none, and its portfolio's decision says so: *hold*, until the theory of change says how new buyers lead to higher earnings.
- **Outcomes and indicators.** Each indicator names how it is read and how often. Readings are kept in their own file, so a new reading each season never rewrites the definition.
- **Components.** The projects and programmes it depends on, each with the reason, shared work written once, loops refused, and the longest chain shown as the critical path.
- **People.** A sponsor, a programme manager and a business change manager, the last being the role that keeps the change in use and sees the benefits arrive after projects hand over. Also a lead team, supporting teams, the mandate it works under, and an escalation route.
- **Risks.** Including what the programme waits on, and escalations.
- **Services.** A service that keeps running names the programmes it belongs to, so the programme can say what carries a benefit on.
- **A decision from outside.** A portfolio holds, for each programme, a priority and a decision to invest, hold or stop, with a reason and a date. The cooperative's portfolio names a review cycle.
- **Advice, never a block.** The programme's checks say what is missing, and never stop it being saved.

Timing forms (dates, windows, waiting on something, set when something happens) are used by projects and by indicators' targets. A programme carries no dates of its own. See [Timing forms](../reference/timing-forms.md).

## What it does not hold today

These gaps are real, and each is named by a source above.

- **A review record on the programme.** When it was last reviewed, by whom, what was decided, and what comes next. A project has an event log and sign-offs. A programme has neither. The portfolio's decision is the funder's view, not the programme's own look at whether it still makes sense.
- **Stages with decision points.** There is no way to say which components are expected before the next decision point, and what will be looked at then. The engine's taxonomy leaves tranches out because scheduling belongs to the delivery tool. The sources describe a tranche as a decision, not a schedule.
- **A reset record.** Nothing says a programme was re-planned, why, or what the earlier plan was.
- **Benefits shown together.** The owner of each indicator, the baseline, when it should start to show, and the work that produces it are all held, but not listed as one register per programme. Only one business change manager is named for the whole programme.
- **Negative effects.** MSP, GovS 002 and the NAO ask for disbenefits and unintended effects. There is no place for them beyond risks.
- **How it ends.** No statement of the conditions under which the programme closes, and what carries the benefit reading on afterwards.
- **A date or trigger on each assumption.** Nothing says what would show an assumption has stopped holding, or when someone looks again.
- **An assurance plan, and programme-level cost.** Money is held on projects and portfolios.

These are open design questions, not promises.
